> > Machine learning Machine learning (ML) is a rapidly expanding and dynamic concept. It is a discipline focused on how machines can be utilized to emulate human capabilities and behaviors. It is viewed as a technological means for addressing queries and extracting significance from data. By amalgamating statistics and computer science, ML enables computers or machines to execute specific tasks. In contrast to humans, ML possesses the ability for swifter learning and adaptation (Berry et al., 2019). As a subfield of artificial intelligence, ML employs techniques, models, and algorithms to learn from data and information, thereby deriving knowledge, identifying patterns, or powering AI applications. ML incorporates a collection of algorithms that serve as methods to train on historical datasets and formulate future predictions. The surge in technology and internet use has led to a rapid generation of data, implying a vast reservoir of information available for machines to learn from and analyze (Joshi, 2019). ML is primarily categorized into three types: supervised learning, unsupervised learning, and reinforcement learning (Berry et al., 2019). Classification Let us consider an output vector denoted as y∈Y, where Y signifies a set of M distinct categories. The primary function of a classifier is to assign data points to these various categories (in other words, to determine to which of the M categories each incoming vector xnew pertains), utilizing the learning gained from experimental data presented within the training dataset X (Gopal, 2018). A variety of algorithms are suitable for this classification duty, including, but not limited to, Decision Trees (Rokach & Maimon, 2008), Support Vector Machines, k-Nearest Neighbor analysis, and Naive Bayes methods (Neapolitan, 2003), alongside others (Cichosz, 2015; Han et al., 2012; Theodoridis, 2015). Unsupervised learning methods In certain machine learning scenarios, information regarding the correlations between input and output characteristics is unavailable. Consequently, the algorithms employed must independently identify similarities or differences within the dataset. This category of methods necessitates a greater degree of human interpretation compared to supervised approaches, as a human decision-maker, whether an individual or a collective, bears the ultimate responsibility for the conclusive judgment. As a result, unsupervised learning is generally categorized into Clustering techniques and Dimensionality Reduction techniques. Among the array of unsupervised approaches, clustering techniques are widely regarded as the most prevalent (Kononenko & Kukar, 2007). Reinforcement learning Reinforcement learning addresses the challenge of instructing an autonomous agent, which actively perceives and interacts with its surroundings, to select the most effective actions to realize its objectives (Kononenko & Kukar, 2007). The agent is provided with data regarding its current situation within the environment and must then leverage its accumulated knowledge—by acting greedily to maximize immediate rewards—while also engaging in exploratory behaviors to identify superior actions for future scenarios (Gopal, 2018; Kononenko & Kukar, 2007). Described with greater formality, reinforcement learning can be represented as a Markov decision process. At any given time step t, based on the prevailing state st (and the associated current reward rt), the agent is tasked with learning a strategy (often termed the “value function") that enables the selection of the optimal decision or action at. This chosen action will subsequently influence the environment, thereby triggering the next reward signal rt+1 (which may be positive, negative, or null) and concurrently leading to the subsequent state st+1. The process of reinforcement learning unfolds through a sequence of trial-and-error iterations until the agent successfully acquires an optimal or near-optimal strategy (Galatzer-Levy et al., 2018).

> > Supervised Learning Supervised learning stands as one of the most prominent methodologies within Machine Learning (ML), characterized by its capacity to learn from past experiences by utilizing a historical dataset and evaluating performance metrics. This approach centers on establishing a mapping from input data to a corresponding target output; it achieves this by learning from the historical dataset what constitutes the most effective way to transform inputs into the desired outputs. It operates using data that is already labeled and, in turn, also generates labeled data as its output (Bzdok et al., 2018). The algorithms employed in supervised learning scrutinize this pre-labeled data, thereby gaining the ability to ascertain the appropriate label that should be assigned to new, unlabeled data, based on the patterns and observations made during the learning phase. The primary techniques employed within supervised learning are classification and regression (Bzdok et al., 2018). Both of these techniques are directed towards constructing predictive models founded on data. Nevertheless, the nature of the target prediction varies according to the specific ML technique applied. In the classification technique, the anticipated output (or target prediction) is categorical or discrete in nature; the model predicts which category an item belongs to from a predefined list. Conversely, the regression technique yields output data that is numerical or continuous. The selection of specific algorithms is contingent upon the type of target prediction (output) desired. A detailed exposition of each algorithm falls outside the purview of this paper. Among the most widely recognized algorithms for each supervised learning technique—be it classification or regression—are the linear classifier, logistic regression, support vector machines (SVM), naive Bayesian (NB) networks, K-means, decision trees, neural networks, and the multi-layer perceptron (Bzdok et al., 2018).
